\begin{abstract}
Claims that AI can forecast scientific breakthroughs require more than plausible ideas or hindsight-selected examples. The future event, information boundary, evidence standard, censoring rule, and dependence among questions must be specified before outcomes are observed. We describe a protocol for freezing theorem- and algorithm-level computer-science events and report a ten-record pilot registry assembled from a qualitative research radar. The registry is selected and heterogeneous, contains no matured labels, and has not undergone independent event review. One frozen event has a deadline/event-date inconsistency, and three earlier records have a pre-2027 observation-window ambiguity. A prior-art audit finds substantial overlap with scientific forecasting benchmarks and, more directly, with a same-project astronomy historical-backtesting protocol that already includes a 200-question prospective instance. We therefore do not establish a novel benchmark, validated evaluation framework, or predictive skill. The current artifact is a protocol proposal and pilot registry. Any future predictive-validity analysis requires independent event review, an adjudication reliability study, family-aware temporal evaluation, and sufficient matured outcomes.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} scientific forecasting; computer science; event registry; temporal validation; evidence adjudication; censoring.

\section{Question and claim boundary}
For event $i$, let $q_i$ be the frozen scientific question, $t_i$ its issue/cutoff time, $d_i$ its deadline, $E_i(t_i)$ its admissible source record, and $g_i$ its research-family identifier. A result is not a match merely because it concerns a similar topic: reviewers must compare the object, assumptions, model, baseline, metric, threshold, quantifiers, version date, and evidence completeness.

The methodological question is whether specific computer-science claims can be frozen for later independent adjudication. This differs from whether an AI system forecasts them accurately. The current evidence does not establish AI forecasting skill, representativeness, calibration, Paper~3 transfer, or Paper~4 planner superiority. Ten selected qualitative items do not form an exchangeable benchmark sample.

\section{Related work and originality}
Science-of-science research has predicted citations, field trends, researcher impact, and proxies for breakthrough papers. For example, Min, Bu, and Sun study early citation-network structure for papers designated as breakthroughs \citep{min2021breakthrough}. Such retrospective bibliometric prediction differs from predicting resolution of a frozen theorem-level question.

\textbf{PreScience} is a scientific forecasting dataset and benchmark built from 98,000 AI papers and a 502,000-paper scholarly graph. It includes temporally snapshotted metadata, seven paper-level or topic-level tasks, baselines from heuristics to language models, and released data/code \citep{ajith2026prescience}. Its targets differ from exact theorem resolution, but its prior existence rules out broad claims such as ``the first scientific forecasting benchmark.''

The closest conceptual priors are Mao's \textit{Evidence-Based Scientific Question Discovery} and \textit{Historical Backtesting for Scientific Question Discovery: A Protocol and Astronomy Pilot}. They freeze source corpora and questions, adjudicate later answers, partial progress, independent posing, ignored status, and premise support/refutation; assess historical cutoffs; study reviewer agreement; and release a 200-question prospective instance \citep{mao2026questiondiscovery,mao2026backtesting}. These are same-project prior work, not independent corroboration. The current CS registry may eventually be a domain extension, but ten unreviewed records do not establish a distinct methodology contribution. ResearchBench evaluates inspiration retrieval and hypothesis composition/ranking; PaperBench evaluates replication rather than future outcome prediction \citep{liu2026researchbench,starace2025paperbench}.

The novelty verdict is \emph{not established}. Freezing events, temporal cutoffs, family grouping, and distinguishing missing evidence from negative outcomes are necessary design choices, not by themselves novel mechanisms. A distinct contribution would require a precise operationalization advance beyond the same-project astronomy protocol and PreScience, external users, transparent curation, reliable adjudication, and a usable released dataset.

\section{Separate outcome targets}
\paragraph{Target A: direct resolution or refutation.} A primary source published after $t_i$ must prove or refute the exact frozen proposition by $d_i$, including scope, assumptions, computational model, metric, and quantifiers. Premise revision is stored separately; it counts as direct resolution only when a frozen rule says it settles the proposition. Nearby results are partial progress.

\paragraph{Target B: evidence trajectory.} This separate extension asks whether substantial, independently verifiable progress occurs without necessarily resolving the question. Candidate dimensions are a verified theorem/bound; matched-baseline algorithmic improvement; independent empirical replication; independent counterexample; premise revision; or substantive scope narrowing. Contradictory evidence, continued uncertainty, and unadjudicated evidence remain distinct. Attention, publication/citation counts, topical similarity, and search-result volume are not progress labels. Search non-detection is missing evidence.

Paper~3's source-native taxonomy is not silently rewritten. The CS Target~B rubric is a separate extension requiring its own human-labeling validation. Historical Paper~3 cohorts and protocols remain unchanged.

\section{Pilot registry and source screening}
The v2 registry contains ten qualitative records: three issued at 2026-10-17 02:47:37 UTC and seven added at 03:15:09 UTC. Topics include planar cut-uncut, discrete Fr\'echet distance, local domination, MAX BISECTION, dynamic matching, ordered Ruzsa--Szemer\'edi growth, exact Subset Sum, block-structured integer programming, shallow-circuit identity checking, and adaptive privacy filters. Dynamic matching and ORS share one family and are not independent units.

The selection report states that 14 additional leads were screened, seven operationalized, and seven excluded. The exclusions and reasons are listed, but the complete row-level inclusion/exclusion evidence, search timestamps and queries, and balanced inclusion rationale are not supplied. The pilot is therefore a selected convenience sample, not a representative sample. No outcomes have matured; no independent event reviews or model comparisons are complete.

Primary-source baselines include the planar cut-uncut theorem \citep{bentert2024twosets}, discrete Fr\'echet upper/lower bounds \citep{bringmann2019frechet}, high-girth domination \citep{lenzen2024domination}, MAX BISECTION \citep{brakensiek2026maxbisection}, dynamic matching and ORS \citep{behnezhad2024dynamic,assadi2025ors}, Subset Sum \citep{chen2023subsetsum,chen2025subsetsum}, block-structured IP \citep{cslovjecsek2025blockip}, shallow-circuit identity checking \citep{bravyi2024identity}, and adaptive $f$-DP filters \citep{tran2026fdp}. The separate technical audit records endpoint-specific comparability obligations and one frozen date defect.

\section{Temporal and endpoint integrity}
The ORS record's structured deadline is 2030-12-31 but its exact event text says ``By 2031-12-31.'' The conflict is preserved, not repaired in place, and prevents unambiguous adjudication. For the original three records, the issue time is in October 2026 but the nominal snapshot observation start is 2027-01-01 and the exact event says ``by deadline.'' A result first public in that intervening period is post-issue evidence under the literal event while preceding the nominal horizon; future adjudication must report both dates and must not rewrite the frozen record.

The cohort stores citation-metadata hashes, not cryptographic checksums of the source-paper contents. A source citation and publication date do not prove which version or full text was reviewed. Source version and theorem locators must be authenticated by human reviewers before labels are finalized.

\section{Evaluation design and validity gates}
Software validity, adjudication reliability, and predictive superiority are separate questions. Software tests establish only schema/hash/append-only behavior. Adjudication requires blinded dual human review of source packets with field-by-field matching, third review for disagreement, and reliability reporting with uncertainty. Predictive superiority requires matured target-specific labels, calibrated forecasts, family-grouped chronological splits, untouched final data, proper scoring, simple baselines, and cluster-aware uncertainty. No such evidence is available.

The frozen repository gates are not lowered to fit this pilot. Target~A requires at least 20 independently adjudicated outcomes with the protocol's class and family support. Target~B has its own larger sample, class, group, and feature-freezing requirements. Publication volume, historical field momentum, and source-only text are appropriate baseline candidates only after adequate labels and a valid evaluation unit exist. Qualitative judgments cannot be retroactively scored as probabilities.

\section{Paper 4 and planning}
Paper~4 is described locally as an offline action planner combining expected information gain, provenance/independence, tractability, and cost. Expected-utility experimental design is established well before this project; Chaloner and Verdinelli review Bayesian design criteria in a unified decision-theoretic framework \citep{chaloner1995bayesian}. The exact Paper~4 paper URL/version is not pinned in the local methods record, so its contribution cannot be independently novelty-audited here. This registry did not freeze matched action sets, execute recommended actions, measure realized information gain, or compare rankings with simple baselines. It gives no evidence of planner utility or superiority.

\section{Limitations, governance, and readiness}
The pilot is selected, small, qualitative, unadjudicated, and not source-content-archived. Its family map is coarse. Monitoring is prepared but inactive. No crawler, worker, scheduled job, or automatic adjudication ran in this audit. No submission or external publication is authorized. The current manuscript is an internal draft, not submission-ready. A future methodology paper should wait for independent event review, complete transparent curation, source provenance and license checks, an adjudication reliability study, and a demonstrated incremental contribution beyond existing scientific forecasting and the same-project astronomy work. Predictive-validity claims belong to a later evaluation after outcomes mature.

\section{Conclusion}
The two-stage research idea is sound in principle: first create a usable and carefully adjudicated event registry, then test prediction skill after outcomes mature. But this pilot alone does not establish a novel benchmark or a publishable methodology contribution. PreScience already occupies broad scientific forecasting benchmark territory, while the same project already has a close historical and prospective scientific-question evaluation protocol. The ten-record CS registry is a useful pilot, not evidence of reliability, benchmark readiness, or AI forecasting ability.
